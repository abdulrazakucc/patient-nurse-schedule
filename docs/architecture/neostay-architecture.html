01

What NeoStay does

The problem, the answer, and who benefits.

A neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) cares for newborn babies who are very small or very ill. Two questions dominate every admission: how long will this baby stay? and how many nurses — and which nurses — will this baby need? NeoStay answers both, using ten years of the unit's own recorded outcomes and a published acuity tool, and presents the answers in a web application a clinician can use in under a minute.

For neonatologists

An expected length of stay with an honest range, the chance of going home, being transferred or dying, and how confident that estimate is — matched to babies of the same birth weight and gestational age.

For charge nurses

A live census that converts each baby's clinical findings into a nurse-to-patient ratio, then adds up the whole unit into nurses needed per shift, the right mix of experience, and a 24-hour roster of the nurses actually available.

For managers and educators

Population analytics across birth-weight bands and gestational weeks, an hourly view of a simulated year of unit activity, and a shared reference for the acuity tool the unit uses.

What NeoStay is not. It is a decision-support prototype for planning and teaching. It is not a medical device, it has not been clinically validated, and it never replaces clinical judgment. It holds aggregated statistics only: no patient-level records exist anywhere in the system, and nothing typed into the tools is sent to a server.

System architecture

Three deployments, one codebase, two engines that must agree.

NeoStay is a web application whose calculations run inside the user's own browser. Nothing a clinician types is transmitted. The server's job is narrower: it checks who is signed in and hands out the reference data. That design keeps the application fast, private, and simple to host — either on a hospital server or on a public static host.

Two engines, kept identical

The same algorithms exist twice: in frontend/engine.js for the browser, and in backend/app/ for the Python API. A test runs the browser engine in a JavaScript runtime and compares its answers with Python's, value by value, so the two can never drift apart (Section 12).

The data foundation

What NeoStay knows, where it comes from, and how it reaches the browser.

NeoStay stands on three bodies of data. None of them contains a patient record. The first is a decade of the centre's own aggregated outcomes; the second is the acuity tool the unit classifies babies with; the third is a synthetic year of unit activity used only for the timeline demonstrations.

Table 4 — The three data sources
SourceContentsUsed by
datasets/losdata/
3 CSV tables		 For each of 11 birth-weight bands and 18 gestational-age groups: how many babies (N), the median stay, and the quartiles Q1 and Q3. A second table splits those by discharge outcome. Prediction (Section 5), Analytics, Home page.
datasets/nurse-skills/
3 CSV tables + scanned PDF		 The Abrazo Arrowhead Neonatal Acuity Tool (11 body systems × 4 ratio columns) and the Nursing Levels of Care (45 general criteria, 19 hyperbilirubinemia criteria). Acuity classifier (Section 7), Scheduling, Acuity tool page.
generated_data/
5 CSV tables		 A deterministic simulation of 709 infants and 42 nurses over a decade, hour by hour, anchored to the real medians and outcome proportions. Timeline page and the hourly API only. Never used for prediction.
The scanned document is never read by the program. The acuity tool arrived as a three-page scan. Its tables were transcribed into CSV files, and only those CSV files are parsed. The handwritten annotations on the scan are deliberately ignored.

How the raw tables are shaped

The outcome tables are "wide": the first row names each band, the second row repeats four sub-columns beneath it (N, Median, Q1, Q3), and each later row is one measure, such as Total Length Of Stay. The loader reads that shape into one statistic per measure per band, and attaches a numeric midpoint to every band so that values can be interpolated between them.

The bands

Predictions are made against 11 birth-weight bands, from under 501 g to over 1400 g, and 18 gestational-age groups, from under 22 weeks to over 37 weeks. Each band carries a midpoint (for example 750 g for the 701–800 g band) which anchors the interpolation in Section 5.

Algorithm 1 — outcome prediction

From two numbers known at birth to a stay, an outcome and a confidence.

In plain words. NeoStay looks up what happened to babies of a similar birth weight, and to babies of a similar gestational age, then blends the two pictures. Because a 720 g baby sits between two recorded weight bands, the figures either side are mixed in proportion, so every gram and every week changes the answer slightly. Nothing is modelled or guessed: the numbers come from the unit's own counts.

The five steps

  1. Locate the bands. The birth weight falls in one weight band (upper bound inclusive), the gestational age in one age group (upper bound exclusive).
  2. Interpolate. For the median, Q1 and Q3, take the two nearest band midpoints and mix them in proportion to the distance. Values beyond the first or last midpoint are held at that end rather than extrapolated.
  3. Blend. Average the weight-based estimate and the age-based estimate.
  4. Discharge outcome. Take the recorded counts of Home, Transfer and Died for that weight band and convert them to percentages. Survival is 100% minus the died percentage.
  5. Confidence. Report how many babies stand behind the answer, graded by sample size.
Interpolation between two band midpoints x₀ and x₁ (values y₀, y₁): y = y₀ + (value − x₀) / (x₁ − x₀) × (y₁ − y₀) Blend of the two views: LOS = mean(LOS by weight, LOS by gestational age) Survival: survival % = 100 − died %

Algorithm 2 — nursing workload for one infant

Turning a forecast stay into nurse-hours, shifts and the experience required.

In plain words. A baby is not equally demanding throughout its stay. The first part is intensive, with one nurse at the cot; later the baby shares a nurse with one other, then with two others as it feeds and grows. NeoStay estimates how sick the baby is, splits the expected stay into those three phases, and adds up the nursing hours.

Step 1 — a severity score between 0 and 1

Two things drive it: how small the baby is and how early it was born. The admission condition and the breathing support then raise it.

weight part = clamp01((1400 − birth weight in g) / 1000) age part = clamp01((34 − gestational age in weeks) / 12) severity (S) = clamp01(0.55 × weight part + 0.45 × age part + modifiers)
Table 6 — Condition modifier
Condition on admissionAdded
Stable0.00
Guarded+0.12
Serious+0.25
Critical+0.40
Table 7 — Breathing support modifier
Respiratory supportAdded
Room air0.00
Nasal cannula+0.05
CPAP+0.15
Ventilator+0.30

Step 2 — split the stay into three phases

intensive share = 0.15 + 0.45 × S → one nurse per baby (1:1) intermediate share = 0.30 + 0.10 × (1 − S) → one nurse per two (1:2) convalescent share = whatever remains → one nurse per three (1:3) (the three shares are normalised so they add to 1)

Step 3 — convert to hours, shifts and a minimum nurse level

phase days = phase share × expected length of stay nurse-hours = phase days × 24 hours × nurses per baby NHPPD = total nurse-hours ÷ length of stay 12-hour shifts = total nurse-hours ÷ 12

The minimum experience level follows the acuity band, then rises for invasive support: severity 0.35 or more requires a Competent nurse (L2), 0.60 or more a Proficient nurse (L3); a ventilated or critically ill baby requires at least L3; a baby that is both critically ill and ventilated requires an Expert (L4). Within a stay, the intensive phase carries the full requirement, the intermediate phase L2, and the convalescent phase L1.

Worked example — the same 720 g baby, serious on admission, on CPAP

Severity reaches its maximum of 1.00 (the small size and early birth already score high, and serious + CPAP add 0.40). With the forecast stay of 102.8 days:

Where the numbers come from. The ratios follow AAP and AWHONN acuity-based staffing standards. The phase shares, the severity weights and the experience thresholds are modelling assumptions for planning discussion, chosen to be easy to change — not values from a validated dataset. Algorithm 3 — the acuity classifier

The unit's own tool, applied consistently to every cot.

In plain words. Instead of estimating how sick a baby is from weight and age, the charge nurse can record what is actually happening at the cot: what breathing support is running, what lines are in, which medicines are infusing. The acuity tool turns those findings into a nurse:patient ratio. A second table, the Nursing Levels of Care, decides which level of nursery the baby belongs to. NeoStay applies both, for every baby, the same way every time.

Findings cover weight and age, respiratory, cardiovascular, A/B/Ds, nutrition, neuro, hyperbilirubinemia, transfusions, infection, lines, and haematology/other. The 64 criteria split into 45 general criteria and 19 hyperbilirubinemia criteria.

Had "Therapeutic Hypothermia/HIE" been ticked instead, the footnote would apply: ratio 1:1, counting as a whole nurse, minimum L4 Expert.

Algorithm 4 — unit staffing and the 24-hour roster

From a census of cots to a defensible shift plan.

In plain words. Each baby needs a fraction of a nurse. Add those fractions together and you have the bedside nurses the shift needs. But a number alone can hide an unsafe roster: three novices cannot replace three experts. So NeoStay also works out how experienced the team must be, then lays the nurses the unit actually has across the day and shows which cots, if any, are left without a qualified nurse.

Step 1 — demand

Every baby contributes its ratio: a 1:1 baby counts 1.00, a 1:2 baby 0.50, a 1:3 baby 0.333. The sum, rounded up, is the number of bedside nurses. One charge nurse is added per shift.

Step 2 — skill mix

Requirements accumulate downward: a nurse may always cover a baby below their level, never above it. NeoStay adds the demand at L4, then L4+L3, then L4+L3+L2, rounding each total up, and converts those running totals into a headcount per level. Two safety rules then apply: novices are capped at 30% of the bedside team, with the surplus promoted to Competent, and every novice on shift needs a Proficient or Expert preceptor. Finally the roster's effective capacity is computed by weighting each nurse by experience, so a numerically adequate but junior shift is visible.

Table 9 — Nurse levels, as NeoStay models them
LevelStageExperienceCounts asTypical assignment
L1Novice / Advanced Beginner0–1 year0.75Convalescent babies; intermediate care with a preceptor.
L2Competent1–3 years0.90Intermediate and special care; stable intensive with support.
L3Proficient3–5 years1.00Intensive 1:1 assignments, including ventilated babies.
L4Expert5+ years1.10Most unstable babies, charge nurse, precepting.

Step 3 — the 24-hour roster

The nurses available across the whole day are dealt out shift by shift, most senior first and rotating, so no shift collects all the experts. Within a shift the most senior nurse becomes charge and carries no cot. The sickest babies are placed first, each into the least senior qualified nurse with room, keeping experts free for the babies who need them. No nurse exceeds one full assignment. If a baby cannot be placed, the charge nurse takes it as a last resort — reported as a warning — and anything still unplaced is listed, with the extra nurses that would close the gap.

The hourly timeline and analytics

What a whole year of this unit looks like, hour by hour.

In plain words. To show how demand rises and falls, NeoStay includes a simulated year of unit activity. It is not real patient data and never feeds a prediction. It is built by drawing babies whose numbers match the real recorded medians, quartiles and discharge proportions, so the shape of the year is realistic while every individual baby is invented.
Table 10 — The simulated unit (deterministic: the same every time it is generated)
MeasureValueHow it is produced
Infants simulated709Counts per birth-weight band follow the real N values.
Nurses simulated42Experience drawn across the four levels, with hire dates.
Average census12.28Babies present, averaged over every hour of the decade.
Peak census23The busiest hour recorded.
Average nurses on duty9.55Rostered from the hourly demand.
Care-phase mix40.8 / 34.3 / 25.0 %Intensive / intermediate / convalescent, across all infant-hours.
Discharge mix553 / 73 / 83Home / transferred / died, drawn using the real proportions.

Lengths of stay are drawn from a log-normal distribution whose median and interquartile range are fitted to the real figures for that band and outcome, then clamped to between 1 and 250 days. The generator is seeded, so reviewers can reproduce the identical dataset and inspect it as plain CSV.

The Analytics page reads the aggregated tables directly and plots length of stay across all 11 weight bands and all 18 gestational-age groups, with the discharge mix per band.

Access control and security

Registered users only — enforced differently on a server and on a static host.

In plain words. Every page begins at a sign-in screen. On a hospital server the server checks the password and refuses to hand over any data until it is satisfied. On a public static host no server exists to do the checking, so the data is published encrypted and the password unlocks it inside the browser. Both use one account list.
Table 11 — Security parameters, as implemented
ControlSetting
Password storagePBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256, 600,000 iterations, random 16-byte salt per account, 32-byte hash
Minimum password length12 characters; the email address itself is rejected
SessionSigned token (HMAC-SHA256) in an HttpOnly, SameSite=Lax cookie, Secure in production, 12 hours by default
Session revocationEvery request re-checks the account and a fingerprint of its password: removing an account or changing its password ends its sessions immediately
Brute-force limits5 failed sign-ins per account and 20 per client in 15 minutes, then a wait
Cross-site sign-inRefused; an unknown email and a wrong password get the same answer, in the same time
Published datagzip, then AES-256-GCM with a fresh 256-bit key per build; key wrapped per account; published files carry no email, name or password hash
Production start-upRefuses to run without sign-in, or with a session secret under 32 characters, or with a wildcard host
Browser policyContent-Security-Policy on every page, X-Frame-Options: DENY, HSTS in production, no caching of data or API responses
Two limits to state plainly. First, anyone can download the published encrypted files and try passwords offline, slowed only by the 600,000 hashing rounds — so accounts used on the public copy need long, unique passwords. Second, removing an account protects only copies published afterwards. A hospital server has neither limitation.

Running NeoStay in a hospital

One container, one address, accounts managed from the command line.

Installation, end to end

# 1 · get the code and set the hospital's address
git clone https://github.com/abdulrazakucc/patient-nurse-schedule.git neostay
cd neostay && cp .env.example .env     # set NEOSTAY_TRUSTED_HOSTS and NEOSTAY_SITE_ADDRESS

Adapting NeoStay to any hospital

Different units grade nurses differently. Here is what fits today, and what does not yet.

NeoStay currently models nurses on Benner's four stages and babies on the N1–N4 levels of care. Hospitals use many other ladders: NHS Agenda for Change bands, clinical ladder tiers I–IV, or local titles such as "junior staff nurse" and "senior sister". Any of these can be used today by mapping local grades onto the four levels; deeper customisation is a code change, and is the first item on the roadmap in Section 14.

A unit with five bands, or with 1:1 policies of its own, would then need no code change.Medium
2 · Load the hospital's own acuity tool The classifier already reads its rules from CSV files. Allowing a hospital to upload its own tool, validated on import, would let each unit classify by its own policy while keeping NeoStay's arithmetic.Medium
3 · Use the hospital's own outcome data Predictions currently rest on one centre's decade. An import step for a unit's own aggregated outcomes — same shape, validated — would make forecasts local, with the sample size shown per band.Medium
4 · Named nurses and real rosters Today the roster places anonymous nurses by level. Naming them, respecting leave, contracted hours, continuity of care and night-shift fairness, is what turns a plan into a publishable roster.Large
5 · Save and reload a unit census The census is lost when the tab closes. Storing it per unit — on the hospital server only — would allow shift handover, a record of what was planned, and later comparison with what happened.Small
6 · Prospective validation Compare forecast stay and forecast staffing with what actually happened, band by band, and publish the error. Without this, NeoStay stays a planning aid; with it, it can be cited.Large
7 · Patient-level modelling With patient-level data and ethical approval, survival and stay could be modelled directly (for example a competing-risks or survival model) instead of interpolated from bands, with uncertainty per baby.Large
8 · Single sign-on everywhere, and an audit trail Proxy-based single sign-on already exists; adding group-based roles (charge nurse, manager, read-only) and an access log would meet most hospital information-governance reviews.Medium
9 · Multi-unit and multi-site view One page comparing several units or hospitals, with per-unit thresholds, would support regional planning and transfers.Medium
10 · Accessibility and offline use A formal accessibility audit (WCAG 2.2 AA) and an offline-capable installable app would make NeoStay dependable on ward computers with poor connectivity.Small
The through-line. Steps 1 to 3 remove every assumption that is specific to one hospital. After those three, adopting NeoStay becomes configuration rather than development — which is the condition for it to be useful to hospitals beyond the one it was written in.
