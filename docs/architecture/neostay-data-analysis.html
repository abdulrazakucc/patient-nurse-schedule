The finding in three sentences. The project holds two real bodies of data: a decade of one centre's aggregated neonatal outcomes, and the unit's acuity instrument transcribed into tables. They are internally consistent and strong enough to describe how length of stay and discharge outcome vary with birth weight and gestation — which is what the application does today. They cannot support a fitted model, a prediction for an individual infant, or any statement about staffing over time, because they contain no individual records, no dates and no workforce data.

The cohort behind the outcome tables

All four outcome files describe the same population: very low birth weight infants admitted to Center 1 between 2016 and 2026, summarised as counts, medians and quartiles. There is no row for an individual infant anywhere, no admission date, and no clinical detail beyond birth weight and gestational age.

An unused document worth reading. The charge nurse task list is the only description the project holds of how a shift is actually run. It names the local artefacts a future data request should ask for by name: the staffing matrix guides used to make assignments, the Monthly Acuity Tracker, the NICU Denominator sheet, the handoff report and the first-admit / float / low-census note written at 15:00. Each is a record the unit already produces, which makes it far cheaper to request than a new collection.
02

How the outcome tables are built

A wide shape, read into tidy rows, then checked against the application.

Each outcome CSV is a wide table. The first row names the groups — birth-weight bands, or gestational-age weeks — and the second row repeats four sub-columns beneath every group. Each later row is one measure.

Table 2 — The shape of every outcome file
RowContents
Row 1Group labels: < 501 g, 501-600 g … > 1400 g, All — each spanning four columns.
Row 2N, Median, Q1, Q3, repeated once per group.
Row 3+One measure per row, for example Total Length Of Stay or Initial Length of Stay (By Disposition) - Died.

The notebook parses this shape into one tidy row per measure and group — 127 statistics in all — using its own parser, deliberately independent of the application's loader. It then compares 40 of those rows against backend/app/data_loader.py. They agree exactly, so the figures in this document and the numbers the application shows come from the same reading of the same files.

Why quartiles matter more than means here. The files publish medians and quartiles, not means or standard deviations. Length of stay is strongly right-skewed — a few infants stay for most of a year — so the median is the honest centre, and the distance from Q1 to Q3 is the only measure of spread available. Every estimate the application makes carries that interquartile band with it rather than quoting a single number.

Structurally the tables are clean. The substantive finding is elsewhere: the measures do not share a denominator.

What this means for modelling. Three admissions have no recorded outcome, four have no total stay, and the predicted-stay measure covers 94 fewer infants than the cohort. With aggregated data there is no way to tell which infants are missing, or whether they differ from the rest. Rates in this document therefore always name the denominator used, and any future model will need the individual records to resolve the gap rather than assume it is random.

The missing values are also unevenly spread: the three unattributed admissions fall in the < 501 g, 501-600 g and > 1400 g bands, one each. That pattern is consistent with infants still admitted when the extract was taken, but the files do not say so, and it is a question worth putting to the centre.

Three readings matter:

  • The gradient is steep and orderly. From 501–600 g to over 1400 g the median stay falls from about four months to six weeks, monotonically, with no reversal.
  • Uncertainty shrinks as weight rises. The interquartile width narrows from 78 days in the 501–600 g band to 17 days at 1301–1400 g: heavier infants are not only quicker to go home, they are more predictable.
  • The smallest band breaks the pattern. At under 501 g the median is the second lowest of all eleven bands, and the interquartile range is the widest at 139 days. Section 5 explains why — and why a model must treat survival and stay together.

Mortality is concentrated at the smallest sizes. Half of the admissions under 501 g did not survive, against roughly one in twenty from 901 g upward. Because a death usually ends an admission early — the median stay for infants who died is 6 days, against 63 days for those discharged home — the smallest band's apparently short stay is a survival effect, not a clinical one.

Why the application reports both together. A single length-of-stay figure averages three different processes: going home, being transferred, and dying. NeoStay shows the discharge mix beside every forecast for exactly this reason, and any fitted model will have to treat these as competing risks rather than as one event.

Above 901 g the remaining variation in mortality (4.3% to 9.5%) rests on three to nine deaths per band. Those counts are too small to read as a trend, and the figure should not be used to rank bands against one another in that range.

Length of stay by gestational age

The same message, with much thinner tails.

The gestational-age view carries the same gradient as the weight view — each additional week before birth shortens the expected stay — but its ends are thin. Five groups hold fewer than ten infants:

Table 7 — Groups too small to estimate separately
GroupInfantsMedian stayReading
< 22 weeks41.0Four infants; almost all die within a day.
35 weeks125.0A single infant. No spread can be estimated.
36 weeks416.5Growth-restricted infants; very low birth weight at 36 weeks is unusual.
37 weeks226.0Two infants.
> 37 weeks224.5Two infants.

The evidence sits in the middle of the distribution: 28 weeks (101 infants), 30 weeks (98), 29 weeks (98) and 25 weeks (81). The application's confidence grade is driven by exactly this count, so a thin group is reported as low-confidence rather than hidden.

A limit worth stating plainly. The weight table and the gestational-age table describe the same 708 infants in two different ways, and no cross-tabulation is published. The joint effect of weight and gestation therefore cannot be measured — only approximated by blending the two margins, which is what NeoStay does and says it does. Resolving it needs individual records.

The predicted-stay measure in the extract

A measure the files carry, which the application does not use.

Alongside the observed figures, the extract carries a measure named Predicted Length of Stay. Its method is not documented in the files. Three observations:

  • It covers a smaller group. 618 infants against the 712 in the disposition table — and the shortfall is largest in the smallest band (15 of 38).
  • It runs high. In ten of eleven bands its median exceeds the observed median, by 6 to 19 days in the bands above 501 g.
  • It diverges sharply at the extreme. In the < 501 g band it gives 141 days against an observed 43.5 — consistent with a measure that projects the stay of a surviving infant and therefore excludes early deaths.
Not used, and deliberately so. NeoStay builds its own forecast from the observed medians and quartiles. This measure is described here because any reader of the raw files will find it, and because asking the centre how it was produced — and on which infants — is a cheap question with a useful answer: if it is a validated vendor model, it becomes a benchmark to compare against.

The acuity instrument as data

A policy, not a sample — and what can still be measured about it.

The second dataset is the unit's own acuity policy — the Abrazo Arrowhead Neonatal Acuity Tool and the Nursing Levels of Care — transcribed from a three-page scan into tables and parsed by backend/app/acuity_tool.py. The scan's handwritten annotations are deliberately ignored.

What cannot be measured. The instrument carries no counts and no outcomes, so nothing held today shows how often each finding occurs, how the case mix distributes across the levels of care, or whether the prescribed ratios match the nursing effort actually spent. That needs the shift-level acuity record (D5) and actual staffing (D6).

What these data support, and what they do not

Four of eleven questions can be answered today.

The pattern is consistent: what the data supports, the application already does — group estimates with honest ranges, outcome mix, evidence counts and acuity-driven staffing. Everything in the "no" column waits on an extract from the hospital, not on development work. The six datasets, their fields and the volumes needed are specified in docs/NeoStay-Data-Requirements.pdf.

The cheapest next step. Three of the four modelling blockers are resolved by one extract: a pseudonymised row per admission with birth weight, gestational age, admission and discharge dates and outcome. That single file converts group estimates into a fitted model, makes competing-risk analysis possible, and gives the joint weight-and-gestation effect that cannot be recovered from the margins.
