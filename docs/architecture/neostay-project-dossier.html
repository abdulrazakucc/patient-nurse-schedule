01

Purpose, goals and success measures

Why the project exists, and how we will know it worked.

Neonatal units plan under two kinds of uncertainty: how long each baby will stay, and how much skilled nursing the unit will need shift by shift. Both are usually answered from memory and experience. NeoStay's purpose is to answer them from the unit's own recorded outcomes and its own acuity policy, in a form a clinician can read in under a minute and a manager can defend in a staffing review.

Aims

Table 1 — Aims and the measurable goals beneath them
AimGoalHow success is measured
Anticipate the journey Give every admission an expected length of stay, a realistic range, and the likely discharge outcome, with an honest confidence signal. Prediction error and calibration against the unit's own subsequent outcomes, reported per birth-weight band (Section 7).
Make staffing defensible Convert the unit's acuity policy into nurses per shift, the required skill mix, and a roster that names gaps before the shift starts. Agreement between NeoStay's recommended establishment and the staffing a senior charge nurse would set, measured on recorded shifts.
Protect skill mix, not just headcount Surface when a shift is numerically adequate but too junior for the babies present. Number of shifts flagged, and whether the flag matched the unit's own retrospective judgement.
Be usable in a busy unit A full census classified and a roster produced in under five minutes, on a ward computer or a phone. Timed task completion with real charge nurses; fewer than two support questions per user in month one.
Travel between hospitals A second site adopts NeoStay by configuration — its own grades, ratios and acuity policy — not by code changes. Time to onboard branch two, and the number of code changes required to do it (target: zero).
Be safe and auditable Every number traceable to a source; access restricted to named accounts; no patient-level data held. Successful information-governance review at the host hospital, and a clean security review.

Who benefits, and how

The baby and family

Earlier, better-informed conversations about likely length of stay, and a unit more reliably staffed to the acuity actually present.

The nursing team

A transparent basis for the shift's establishment and skill mix, and an explicit record of what was requested when cover was short.

The hospital

Objective evidence for establishment reviews, bank and agency decisions, and cross-branch comparison of demand.

A deliberate limit. NeoStay recommends; it never assigns. Final decisions on cot allocation, escalation and individual nurse assignment rest with the charge nurse and the consultant on duty. The interface is written so that this is never ambiguous.

Scope, and what this project is not

Boundaries agreed before any data arrives.

In scope

  • Forecasting length of stay, discharge outcome and survival for very low birth weight infants.
  • Translating an acuity policy into nurse:patient ratios and minimum nurse competency.
  • Unit-level demand, skill mix and a 24-hour roster of the nurses actually available.
  • Population analytics across every birth-weight band and gestational week.
  • Multi-branch deployment, per-branch configuration, and roll-up reporting.

Out of scope

  • Diagnosis, treatment recommendation, drug dosing, or any individual clinical decision.
  • Storing patient-identifiable records, notes or images.
  • Replacing the hospital's rostering or payroll system of record.
  • Automatic assignment of named nurses without human approval.
  • Regulatory medical-device claims (see Section 13 for the boundary and what would cross it).

Principles that constrain every design decision

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Traceability before clevernessEvery figure can be traced to a source row, a policy line, or a stated assumption. A model that cannot explain a number does not ship.
Aggregate by defaultThe product runs on aggregated statistics. Patient-level data, where it is used for model development, stays inside the hospital's governance boundary.
Local policy winsRatios, grades, supervision rules and levels of care are the hospital's, not the software's.
Degrade honestlyLow sample size, missing data or an unfamiliar case is shown as such, never smoothed into false confidence.
Portable by constructionOne container, environment-variable configuration, no managed cloud service required.

Models to be built and tested

Shortlists, with the reason each is a candidate and the evidence that would retire it.

Method, in one paragraph. For each problem we start from the simplest model that could work, add complexity only when it earns its place on held-out data, and prefer models whose output can be explained at the cot side. Every candidate is scored on the same split, against the same baseline, with the same reporting checklist. A model that wins on error but cannot be calibrated or explained does not go into the product.

P1 · Length of stay and discharge outcome

Table 4 — Candidate models for P1
Model familyWhy it is a candidateMain risk
Parametric survival (AFT: log-normal, Weibull) Length of stay is a time-to-event with censoring (still inpatient at extract). AFT gives a median and quantiles directly, which is what the interface already shows. Distributional assumption may not hold across weight bands.
Cox proportional hazards Standard, well-understood, no distributional assumption; good for effect sizes and for clinical review of which variables matter. Needs a baseline hazard to produce days; proportionality often violated by respiratory support.
Competing-risks (Fine–Gray, cause-specific hazards) Discharge home, transfer and death compete. Treating death as censoring biases stay estimates upward — the clinically important correction. More parameters, needs enough events in each outcome.
Multi-state model Represents the real pathway: intensive → intermediate → convalescent → discharge, with readmission to a higher level. Produces P1 and P2 from one structure. Data hungry; needs reliable daily level-of-care records.
Gradient boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM) with survival objective Handles non-linearity and interactions (weight × respiratory support) without hand-crafting; strong tabular baseline. Needs calibration and explanation work; risks fitting one unit's practice.
Quantile regression / quantile forests The product shows a range, not a point. Modelling Q1 and Q3 directly optimises exactly what is displayed. Quantile crossing; less familiar to clinical reviewers.
Bayesian hierarchical model Partial pooling across branches: a small unit borrows strength from the group without losing its own signal. Natural uncertainty intervals. Slower to fit; requires care in prior choice and explanation.
Conformal prediction wrapper Distribution-free intervals with a guaranteed coverage rate, on top of whichever model wins. Intervals can be wide; needs a clean calibration set.

P2 · Acuity trajectory

Model familyWhy it is a candidate
Markov / multi-state transition modelCare level is a small ordered state space; transition probabilities per shift are directly interpretable and easy to audit with the clinical lead.
Ordinal logistic regressionRespects the ordering N1 < N2 < N3 < N4 and gives odds ratios clinicians can challenge.
Gradient boosting with cost-sensitive lossUnder-calling acuity is more harmful than over-calling; the loss can be weighted accordingly.
Sequence model (GRU/temporal convolution)Only if daily records prove rich enough; held as a stretch option, never as the first choice.

P3 · Unit demand forecasting

Model familyWhy it is a candidate
Seasonal naive and moving averageThe baseline every other model must beat; often strong for ward census.
SARIMA / state-space (ETS, structural time series)Captures weekday and seasonal patterns with interpretable components and prediction intervals.
Gradient boosting on calendar and census featuresEasily absorbs admissions pipeline, bed closures, school holidays, local events.
Queueing approximation (Erlang / M/M/c)Converts a demand distribution into a staffing level at a stated risk of shortfall — the question management actually asks.
Discrete-event simulationStress-tests a proposed establishment against arrival and length-of-stay distributions; answers "what if two cots close".
Selection rule, agreed in advance. A candidate replaces the incumbent only if it improves the primary metric on a temporal hold-out and on at least one external branch, keeps calibration within agreed bounds, produces an explanation the clinical lead accepts, and runs inside the interaction budget (Section 12). Otherwise the simpler incumbent stays.

P4 · Roster optimisation

ApproachWhy it is a candidate
Constraint programming / integer programming (CP-SAT, MILP)Hard rules (competence, ratios, one assignment per nurse, charge nurse free) as constraints; soft goals (continuity of care, fairness of nights, preferences) as weighted objectives. Produces a provably feasible roster or a precise reason none exists.
Greedy heuristic (today's algorithm)Instant, explainable, already in production; remains the fallback when the solver cannot finish inside the interaction budget.
Local search / simulated annealingImproves a feasible roster within a time budget; useful for large units with many soft preferences.

Validation, governance and monitoring of models

How a candidate earns its place, and how it is watched afterwards.

Validation protocol

  1. Split by time, not at random. Train on the earlier period, test on the most recent 12 months, so the evaluation answers "would it have worked last year".
  2. Hold out a branch. With more than one site, leave-one-branch-out tells us whether the model transfers or has learned one unit's habits.
  3. Report discrimination and calibration together. Concordance or balanced accuracy alone is not enough: a calibration plot and slope decide whether numbers can be shown to clinicians.
  4. Quantify the interval, not just the point. Pinball loss and empirical coverage for every published range; conformal intervals where coverage must be guaranteed.
  5. Compare against the incumbent. Every metric is reported beside today's interpolation or rule, with a confidence interval on the difference.
  6. Clinical review of errors. The clinical lead reviews the worst 20 predictions, not just the averages. A systematic failure mode blocks adoption regardless of mean error.
  7. Prospective silent run. The model runs beside the unit for an agreed period, recording what it would have said, with no influence on care, before anything is displayed.

Reporting and governance

Reporting standards

Model development and validation reported to TRIPOD+AI; risk of bias assessed with PROBAST; any prospective evaluation follows DECIDE-AI. Each released model carries a one-page model card: intended use, data, performance by subgroup, known failure modes, and the date it must be reviewed.

Change control

Models are versioned artefacts with the data snapshot that produced them. A model change follows the same review, test and approval path as a code change, and the clinical lead signs the model card before release.

Monitoring in service

Input drift (case mix, missingness), output drift (distribution of predictions), and realised error once outcomes are known. Alert thresholds agreed per branch; breach triggers review, not silent retraining.

Human factors

Predictions are shown with their range and confidence, never as a single authoritative number. The interface requires an explicit human action for every staffing decision, and records that the recommendation was advisory.

Fairness and equity. Performance is reported by gestational-age band, birth weight, inborn versus outborn, and branch. A model that is materially worse for the smallest babies, or for one site, is not released even if its average is better.

How each role uses NeoStay

Six roles, each with the task they come to do and the path they take.

Role 1 · Charge nurse / shift coordinator — the primary user

"How many nurses do I need tonight, and who should take which cot?"

When: at each shift handover, and whenever a cot changes acuity. Time budget: under five minutes for a full unit.

  1. Sign in; the unit's census from the previous shift is the starting point.
  2. For each cot, tick the most intensive finding per body system, the 1:1 box if the baby meets the footnoted criteria, and the highest level-of-care criterion met. The preview shows the ratio, care level and minimum nurse level before the cot is added.
  3. Enter the nurses actually available by grade, and the shift pattern.
  4. Read the verdict: nurses per shift, recommended skill mix, effective capacity against demand, and the 24-hour roster. Any cot without a qualified nurse is named in red, with the grade and number of extra nurses that would close the gap.
  5. Export or record the decision; escalate with the figure if cover is short.
Role 2 · Neonatologist / consultant

"What should I tell these parents, and what will this baby need?"

Opens Predict, enters birth weight, gestational age, condition and respiratory support. Reads the expected stay with its realistic range, the chance of going home, transfer or death, the survival figure, and the confidence grade with the number of comparable babies behind it. Uses the per-phase nursing profile when planning cot moves and discharge preparation. Every figure carries the caveat that it describes groups, not this baby.

Role 3 · Unit manager / matron

"Is my establishment right, and what do I take to the staffing review?"

Runs the unit's typical census through the staffing model to compare recommended establishment with actual staffing; collects the record of shifts where cover was short and by which grade. Once the hospital supplies its own census history (D4), the same comparison runs over a real period. The output is evidence for an establishment case, not a rota.

Role 4 · Practice educator

"Who needs supervision, and where are my skill gaps?"

Reads the skill-mix panel's preceptor requirement and novice share across shifts, and the Acuity tool page as a teaching reference for the unit's own policy.

Role 5 · NeoStay administrator (nursing or informatics)

"Who has access, and is the service healthy?"

Creates and removes accounts, resets passwords, checks the health endpoint and the service status. Account changes take effect immediately; removing an account signs that person out everywhere.

Role 6 · Hospital IT / platform team

"How do I run, secure, upgrade and back this up?"

Runs one container behind the hospital's HTTPS entry point, supplies five environment variables, backs up the accounts file, applies upgrades by deploying a new image tag, and monitors /api/health. Hospital single sign-on replaces NeoStay passwords where required.

Design and accessibility standards

The rules the interface is held to, and why they matter at 03:00.

Clarity under pressure

One primary action per screen. Numbers before adjectives. Plain verdicts in words ("fully covered", "short by 0.1 nurses") beside every chart, so a decision never depends on reading a graph correctly.

Traceability

Every derived number can be hovered or tapped to see what produced it — the findings that set a ratio, the sample size behind a forecast.

No silent failure

Missing data, low confidence, an expired session or a browser that cannot decrypt the published copy each produce a specific message and a next step.

Accessibility

Target WCAG 2.2 AA: contrast, focus visibility, full keyboard operation, labelled controls, text that scales to 200%, and no meaning carried by colour alone. Formal audit is a roadmap item.

Works on ward hardware

Responsive from 360 px upward; no dependency on hover; touch targets sized for gloved hands; fonts fall back cleanly if external fonts are blocked.

Consistency

One design system across landing page, sign-in and the application, so the product feels like one tool rather than three.

Usability engineering. For a clinical tool, usability is a safety property. The project follows the spirit of IEC 62366-1: named user groups, defined use scenarios, identification of use errors that could cause harm (for example a misread ratio), and testing with real users before wide release.

Installing across a multi-branch hospital group

The part most often underestimated: several units, one service, different local policies.

Choose a tenancy model first

Table 8 — Three ways to serve several branches
ModelHow it worksBest whenTrade-off
A · One deployment per branch Each unit runs its own container and its own accounts; no shared data. Branches are independent legal entities, or governance forbids any pooling. No group-level view; upgrades repeated per site.
B · One deployment, branch-scoped configuration recommended One service; each user belongs to a branch; policy, grades and ratios are per branch; data stays separated by branch. A group with shared IT and a wish to compare units. Requires the configuration work in Section 15, Phase 2.
C · Central service, federated data Branches keep their own data; only aggregated statistics travel to the central service. Cross-border groups, or where data residency differs by site. Most complex; needed only if data cannot be centralised.

What must be configured per branch

Clinical policy

  • Nurse grade ladder and the mapping to the four levels
  • Ratios for each acuity column, and the local 1:1 criteria
  • Levels of care in use, and the mapping to minimum nurse level
  • Supervision rules: novice cap, preceptor requirement
  • Shift pattern (two 12-hour, three 8-hour, local variants)

Operational

  • Branch name, designated unit level, cot capacity
  • Accounts and roles, by branch
  • Host name and certificate
  • Which dataset the branch's forecasts use — its own, or the group's pooled statistics
  • Support contacts and escalation path

Installation checklist (per branch)

Before install

  • Information-governance approval recorded
  • Tenancy model agreed (A, B or C)
  • Host name allocated; TLS certificate issued
  • Nurse grade mapping signed off by the branch's nursing lead
  • Acuity policy confirmed, or the group policy adopted explicitly
  • Named administrator and deputy identified
  • Account list agreed with line managers

Install and verify

  • Container deployed; health check green
  • HTTPS verified from a ward computer and a phone
  • First administrator account created; password delivered securely
  • End-to-end smoke test passed and recorded
  • Branch configuration applied and reviewed
  • Backup of the accounts file tested, including restore
  • Monitoring and alerting connected

Go live

  • Two training sessions delivered per shift pattern (day and night)
  • Quick-reference card at each ward computer
  • Two weeks of parallel running against the current manual method
  • Daily check-in during week one; weekly thereafter
  • Feedback log open, with named owner

Steady state

  • Quarterly review of policy configuration with the nursing lead
  • Access list reviewed every six months
  • Upgrades applied within one month of release
  • Incident and near-miss log reviewed with the clinical lead
  • Annual revalidation of forecasts against that branch's outcomes
The three mistakes that derail multi-site rollouts. First, assuming one branch's ratios are everyone's — they are not, and the mapping must be signed per site. Second, creating accounts centrally without a local owner, so leavers keep access. Third, going live without parallel running, which removes the only opportunity to compare the recommendation with what the unit would have done anyway.

Scaling as usage grows

From one unit to a hospital group, without re-architecting.

NeoStay's calculations run in the browser, so user growth costs the server almost nothing: each additional user adds a sign-in check and a few static file requests. The scaling work is therefore about availability, data volume and the features that will add server-side state.

Table 9 — Capacity plan
StageUsersShapeWhat changes
PilotUp to 50One container, one replica, 0.5 vCPU, 512 MBNothing — today's deployment.
One hospital50–3002–3 replicas behind the hospital's load balancer; static files cached at the edgeStateless mode with a shared session secret; health-based autoscaling.
Group of branches300–2,000Replicas per region or per branch; shared configuration serviceBranch-scoped configuration and roles; a managed Postgres for saved censuses and audit; rate limiting moved to a shared store.
Group with analytics2,000+Separate read path for reporting; scheduled model retraining jobsA warehouse for historical data; a job runner for forecasts; per-branch dashboards.
Known constraint to fix before large scale. The limit on failed sign-ins is counted inside each server process, so several replicas allow proportionally more attempts. Moving that counter to a shared store is a prerequisite for an internet-facing deployment at group scale.

Performance budget

OperationBudgetWhy that number
Sign-in (server check)< 500 msPerceived as instant at handover.
Census recalculation after a change< 100 msRuns locally; must feel immediate while a nurse ticks findings.
Roster for 30 cots and 40 nurses< 1 s heuristic; < 5 s solverBeyond this, the planner stops trusting the tool and uses paper.
Forecast refresh (server job)< 15 min nightlyMust finish well before the early shift.

What stays true as it grows

  • Stateless web tier. Sessions are signed tokens, so any replica serves any user.
  • One image everywhere. Branch differences are configuration, never a separate build.
  • Data stays small. Statistics and policy files are kilobytes; only the optional historical warehouse grows, outside the request path.
  • Graceful degradation. If a model service is down, the deterministic method still answers, labelled as such.

The regulatory boundary. NeoStay today informs staffing and planning: it makes no diagnostic or treatment claim and is therefore not a medical device. Three changes would cross that line: predicting an individual baby's clinical outcome for clinical management, driving treatment decisions automatically, or marketing it for diagnosis or prognosis. If the project chooses to cross it, a quality management system (ISO 13485), software lifecycle process (IEC 62304), clinical evaluation and regulatory submission become mandatory, and the roadmap must be replanned accordingly.

Scope creep towards a medical deviceUnplanned regulatory burden.Device boundary stated (Section 13); any proposal that crosses it triggers a formal decision, not a quiet implementation.

Delivery plan

Three phases, each with an exit test.

Phase 1 · FoundationsConfigure, pilot, measure

Branch-level configuration of grades, ratios and policy; accessibility and security audits; hazard log opened; pilot in one unit with parallel running; usage and task-time measured.

Exit test: one unit uses NeoStay for every shift for four weeks, with no safety incident and a majority of charge nurses preferring it to the manual method.

Phase 2 · EvidenceData, models, validation

Patient-level extract under governance; P1 and P3 models built and compared with the deterministic baseline; silent prospective run; TRIPOD+AI report; model cards reviewed and signed.

Exit test: a model beats the incumbent on the primary metric and on calibration, in a temporal hold-out and in one external branch.

Phase 3 · ScaleGroup rollout

Branch-scoped multi-tenancy; roster optimisation with constraints; saved censuses and audit; single sign-on; dashboards per branch; annual revalidation cycle established.

Exit test: a second and third branch onboarded by configuration only, each inside two weeks.

A · Glossary of methods named in this document

TermPlain meaning
Survival model (AFT, Cox)A model for "how long until something happens" that copes with cases still ongoing when the data was extracted.
Competing risksMore than one way the stay can end. Treating death as if the baby were simply still inpatient would overstate length of stay.
Multi-state modelA model of movement between care levels, rather than a single endpoint.
CalibrationWhether predicted numbers match observed reality on average: if the tool says 100 days, do such babies stay about 100 days?
Concordance (C-index)Whether the model ranks babies correctly: does the one it says will stay longer actually stay longer?
Pinball lossA score for a predicted range rather than a single number.
Conformal predictionA method that produces ranges with a guaranteed hit rate, whatever model sits underneath.
Hierarchical (partial pooling)Letting a small unit borrow strength from the group while keeping its own signal.
Constraint programmingDescribing the rules a roster must obey and letting a solver find an assignment that satisfies them.
DriftThe world changing after a model was built — different case mix, different practice — so its accuracy decays.
